Executive summary
We build ClearScript: an agent that turns a faxed prescription into a verified, structured
order in under 30 seconds, and refuses to guess. Every field carries its evidence (the
pixels it came from) and a calibrated confidence. Anything uncertain goes to a
pharmacist, and the agent can close the loop itself by calling the prescriber's office to
confirm.
The hack-winning angle is not "OCR with an LLM." Every team will do that. Ours is three
things judges can see:
1. Calibrated uncertainty, measured. We report a silent error rate (wrong fields we did
not flag) on a 200-fax synthetic benchmark, against a naive single-pass LLM baseline.
That one number is the pitch.
2. Closed-loop clarification. A flagged field becomes a one-click outbound call to the
prescriber's office through an ElevenLabs voice agent. The confirmed answer is
written back into the order with the call transcript as evidence.
3. Every decision is auditable. Each extraction, lookup and rule check is traced through
Block Convey, so a pharmacist or a state board inspector can replay why a field was
accepted or flagged.
The demo proves one claim: on the faxes where a naive pipeline silently ships a 10x dosing
error, ClearScript stops and asks.
Problem and users
Faxed prescriptions are retyped by hand, and the dangerous failure is not a slow entry but
a confident wrong one. A technician who misreads 5 mg as 50 mg, or hydroxyzine as
hydralazine, produces an order that looks clean and passes every downstream screen.

What is hard about faxes
200 dpi or lower, 1-bit, skewed, with streaks and toner noise.
Mixed layouts: EHR-printed forms, handwritten pads, multi-Rx pages, cover sheets.
Handwritten numerals and drug names, where one stroke flips meaning.
Abbreviated directions ("1 po bid x10d prn") that must be parsed, not copied.
Open question for the PillSpark founder at kickoff: which pharmacy system do orders land
in, and what does their current error or callback rate look like?
Product: fax to verified order
A fax becomes a verified order in seven steps, and a human touches it only where the
system is unsure.
1. Fax arrives. Through an eFax webhook, email-to-fax inbox or manual upload (PDF or
TIFF). Each page becomes a FaxJob with a status.
2. Pages are cleaned and split. Deskew, denoise, upscale. A classifier drops cover sheets
and splits multi-prescription pages into one order each.
3. Three independent reads. Three different vision models read the page through the
Nicrron gateway, and AWS Textract reads it in parallel. Each returns every field with the
exact text it saw and where it saw it.
4. Values are normalized against real registries. Drug to RxNorm, prescriber to the NPI
Registry, directions to a structured sig, dates and DOB to ISO.
5. Rules check the order as a whole. Dose range, quantity against days supply, look-alike
drug names, DEA checksum, controlled-substance limits.
6. Each field is triaged. Green fields are pre-accepted, yellow need a glance, red block
the order. Every flag shows a one-line reason.

OpenCV: deskew by Hough line angle, median denoise, adaptive threshold, 2x upscale
for small text.
Keep both the cleaned and the original image. Models read the cleaned one; the UI
shows the original, because a pharmacist trusts what was actually sent.
Page classifier (one cheap vision call): cover_sheet | prescription |
multi_prescription | other . Multi-Rx pages get one order per prescription block.
3. Extract ( extract/ )
Three model reads, in parallel, through the Nicrron OpenAI-compatible gateway: for
example a Claude, a GPT and a Gemini vision model. Different model families make
different mistakes, so agreement among them means more than three runs of one
model.
Each call uses strict JSON output against the schema and must return text_seen and
a bounding box for every field. A missing evidence string counts as no read.
Textract read with AnalyzeDocument (FORMS) on the bytes, synchronously. Keep
per-word confidence scores. Map key-value pairs to fields with the label aliases from
the prior repo.
Prompt rule: "If you cannot read a character, output ? in its place. Never infer a value
from context." This turns illegibility into a visible signal.
4. Normalize ( normalize/ )
Drug: RxNav approximateTerm and getDrugs to RxNorm concept (RxCUI),
ingredient, strength, dose form. Record the match score.
Prescriber: NPI Registry API by NPI, then by name + state. Compare name, address and
phone with the fax.
DEA: format and checksum (sum of digits 1, 3, 5 + 2 × sum of digits 2, 4, 6; last digit
must equal the final digit of that total). Second letter should match the prescriber's
last-name initial.
Sig: an LLM parse into {dose, unit, route, frequency_per_day, duration_days,
prn, indication} plus a deterministic expander for common Latin abbreviations (po,
bid, tid, qhs, prn). Both must agree.
Dates: dateutil , rejecting ambiguous day/month orders instead of guessing.
5. Verify ( verify/ ) — rules that look at the whole order. Each rule returns pass, warn or
fail with the fields it touches.

Scoring
A small logistic regression turns the signals into a probability that the field is correct,
trained on the synthetic benchmark where ground truth is known. Then isotonic
calibration on a held-out split. Simple, explainable, and every coefficient can be shown to a
judge.
P(\text{correct}) = \sigma\left(w_0 + w_1 \cdot \text{agree}_{\text{models}}
+ w_2 \cdot \text{agree}_{\text{ocr}} + w_3 \cdot \text{conf}_{\text{ocr}} +
w_4 \cdot \text{match}_{\text{registry}} - w_5 \cdot \text{illegible}\right)
Triage thresholds (per risk class, tuned on the benchmark)
Green: P(correct) ≥ 0.99 for high-risk fields, ≥ 0.97 for others, and no failing rule.
Yellow: below green, or any warn-level rule.
Red: any red rule, any illegible high-risk field, or reads disagree on drug name or
strength.
Hard stops (never auto-green, whatever the score)
Strength or drug name where the 3 model reads are not unanimous.
Any Schedule II drug: every field at least yellow, pharmacist must touch it.

Nicrron: the ensemble
Point the OpenAI SDK at https://api.nicrron.ai/v1 . Three calls, three model
families, one client.
The pitch line: "three independent readers from three labs; we trust a strength only
when all three agree." Different labs mean less correlated errors.
Gateway failover keeps the pipeline up if one provider stalls. Its usage log gives cost
per fax, which goes in the business slide.
Verify at kickoff that vision input passes through the gateway for the models we pick;
their site lists chat and image models but does not say so explicitly. Fallback: Claude on
Bedrock.
ElevenLabs: closing the loop
An ElevenLabs agent with a prompt scoped to one job: confirm specific prescription
fields with a prescriber's office, never give medical advice, never accept changes to
anything not asked about.
The backend calls POST /v1/convai/twilio/outbound-call with agent_id ,
agent_phone_number_id , to_number , and the question passed in
conversation_initiation_client_data.dynamic_variables (pharmacy name,

Production posture (what we tell the founder)
BAAs before PHI: AWS (Textract, S3, Bedrock) is HIPAA-eligible under a BAA. Each
model provider behind the gateway, ElevenLabs, Twilio and the tracing vendor need a
BAA or must receive no PHI. Until then, route PHI-bearing calls to Bedrock only.
Minimum necessary: the voice agent says patient initials and drug, not full name and
DOB, unless the office asks to verify identity. Tavily queries carry only public
prescriber and drug data.
Encryption: TLS in transit; S3 and Postgres encrypted at rest with KMS keys.
Access: SSO with role-based access (technician, pharmacist, admin). Only a
pharmacist can approve.
Audit trail: every view, edit, call and approval is an append-only AuditEvent with user
and timestamp, kept for the retention period pharmacy boards require. Block Convey
traces mirror it for debugging, with PHI redacted from span attributes.
Human in the loop by design: the system never dispenses or transmits an order on its
own. A pharmacist approves every order, which keeps it decision support rather than
an autonomous system.
Retention: fax images kept per state pharmacy rules, then deleted on schedule.
Open question for the founder: which pharmacy states and systems they serve first, since
record-retention rules vary by state.
Startup readiness
The wedge is the moment between fax arrival and pharmacist verification; the moat is the
labeled correction data every pharmacist edit creates.
Wedge: independent and small-chain pharmacies that still receive a large share of
prescriptions by fax and have no data-entry team. We sit in front of their existing
pharmacy system and hand it a clean order, so there is no rip-and-replace.
Why now: vision models finally read messy handwriting well enough, and an ensemble
with calibrated confidence makes it safe to rely on them where it matters.
Pricing (proposal to test): per processed fax, with a monthly minimum per store. Bundles
a number of voice clarification calls. The Nicrron usage log gives our cost per fax, so gross
margin is known from day one.

Go-to-market
1. Design partners through PillSpark's existing pharmacy relationships.
2. Integrations with the pharmacy management systems those stores use, starting with
file import, then direct API.
3. Case study built on measured outcomes: tech minutes per order, callbacks avoided,
errors caught.
Moat
Every pharmacist correction is a labeled example of exactly where models fail on real
faxes. That data retrains the scorer and, later, a fine-tuned reader. Competitors
without the review loop cannot collect it.
Per-prescriber priors: once a clinic's form layout and a doctor's handwriting have been
seen, accuracy on their next fax goes up.
The audit trail makes it the system of record for how each order was verified, which is
sticky.
Roadmap after the hackathon
1. Pilot: one pharmacy, shadow mode (we process in parallel, techs still type), measure
accuracy on real faxes.
2. Assisted: techs review our pre-filled orders; thresholds tuned on their corrections.
3. Closed loop: voice clarification calls in production; e-prescribing (NCPDP SCRIPT)
intake alongside fax so one queue covers both.
4. Expand: refill requests, prior authorization forms, and lab orders use the same read,
verify, flag pipeline.
Demo script and judge Q&A
Five minutes, four speakers, one story: the naive pipeline ships a 10x error, ClearScript
stops and asks, the doctor's office confirms on a live call.
Script
1. Hook (D, 30 s). "This fax says metformin. Is it 500 or 850? A naive LLM pipeline picks
one with total confidence. Here's what it shipped." Show the naive output with the
wrong strength, no warning.
2. Clean fax (A, 45 s). Drop a clean fax into the queue. It processes in seconds, every
field green, each traceable to its box on the image. Approve. FHIR JSON appears.

3. The dangerous fax (B, 75 s). Drop the smudged one. Strength goes red: "2 of 3 readers

your pharmacies next week."

Likely judge questions
Question Answer
Why not one strong model? Our ablation shows how many silent errors one
model lets through; three labs disagreeing is the
uncertainty signal.
Isn't calling the doctor slow? It happens only on red fields, starts with one click,
and replaces the call the pharmacist would make
anyway.
How is confidence calibrated? Logistic regression over measurable signals,
isotonic calibration on held-out faxes; we show the
reliability curve.
Synthetic data only? Yes, with traps modeled on known error types; first
pilot step is shadow mode on real faxes.
HIPAA? Synthetic data now; production keeps PHI with
BAA-covered vendors and logs every access.
Cost per fax? Measured from the gateway usage log; shown on
the slide.
